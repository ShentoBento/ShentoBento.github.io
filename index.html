What they are

The Velvet Pro board is a drop-in solder PCB for the Velvet Pro, a keyboard by Ampersandy, who supported the project and gave me the board outline. The Uni60 reuses its matrix and MCU routing and turns it into a universal 60% board: ANSI and ISO, split backspace, split left and right shift, stepped caps, and five bottom-row layouts. I re-routed the matrix around tray-mount holes drilled between the keys so it also fits tray-mount cases.

Both boards use an STM32F072, get USB through a separate daughterboard over a 4-pin cable, and run QMK with VIA support, so owners can remap keys without compiling firmware.

The connector problem

Custom cases route the daughterboard cable through a channel, and every case puts that channel somewhere different. A JST-SH connector is tall enough to hit the case floor anywhere outside the channel. I placed five daisy-chained JST-SH positions where popular cases' channels line up, and builders clip off the ones they don't need. A sixth option, a Molex Pico-EZmate, sits lower than the switch pins on the back of the board, so it fits any case.

Production and testing

I ran two assembled batches through JLCPCB: 15 Velvet Pro boards in April 2026 and 50 Uni60s in September 2026. I made the fabrication and assembly files, picked the parts, and packed and shipped every order myself. Before shipping, I flashed each board with QMK and shorted every row and column to confirm the MCU could read the whole matrix.

That test doesn't catch a single bad diode or a broken trace to one key. A per-key check is what I'd add to the next run.

Aluminum keyboard case

2026
Solo · SolidWorks · 6063 aluminum · CNC machining and anodizing, sourced overseas

A 60% case for my own boards. It's an O-ring mount, meaning the plate and PCB are held in the case by a rubber O-ring instead of being screwed down, with a brass weight in the bottom. I found a manufacturer to CNC-machine the case and plates from 6063 aluminum and anodize them, and made two units: one for me and one for a friend.

Fixing the ping

Typing on v1 made a metallic ping. I traced it to the weight: it's long and was held by only two M3 screws, so its unsupported middle vibrated against the case. Rev 2 (five units) holds it with six screws. Once rev 2 is made, I want to record typing on both versions and compare their spectra.

Multi-driver in-ear monitors In progress

May 2026 – present
Solo · KiCad · SolidWorks · IEC-711 coupler · Rev 2 funded by IEEE at UCLA

In-ear monitors with four balanced-armature drivers per side, each covering part of the frequency range. I designed the crossover, a small PCB shaped to fit inside the shell with one filter branch per driver, and the resin shell in SolidWorks.

For rev 1, I measured each driver's frequency response on an IEC-711 coupler and chose filter values to pull the combined response toward a target curve: a measurement of an IEM I like, after EQ.

How rev 1 turned out

Not well. The final response was 3 to 5 dB off the target and didn't sound good. Two things went wrong. Balanced-armature drivers don't behave like resistors: their impedance changes a lot with frequency, so a filter calculated for a fixed load crosses over in the wrong place. And the acoustic low-pass channels I tried printing into the resin shell collapsed and came out at uneven sizes. I also didn't keep good records of rev 1, so there's no measurement plot here yet.

Rev 2

Rev 2 is funded by a $150 grant from IEEE at UCLA's Student Project Initiative. This time I'm modeling each driver's measured impedance in LTspice before picking parts, aiming to land within ±2 dB of the target, and documenting the build as I go.

Tendon-driven bionic hand

Fall 2025
Team of 3 · UCLA engineering course · SolidWorks · 3D printing

A 3D-printed hand on a forearm. Five servos inside the forearm each pull a fishing-line tendon that curls one finger. I designed the hand and forearm in SolidWorks; my teammates wrote the Arduino code.

The hard part was packing five servos into the forearm so it could still be assembled, routing five tendons so they never cross, and shaping the tendon channels so they printed without collapsing. I set the finger joint geometry so the thumb and index finger meet at full closure. In the final build all five fingers moved, and the hand could pinch grape-sized objects.

Solar-tracking weather station

Apr 2026
Team of 5 · IDEA Hacks 2026, a 36-hour hackathon · laser cutting · servo mechanism

A weather station that turns its solar panel toward the sun. I did the body and the moving parts: a laser-cut plywood enclosure with finger joints, and a servo-driven acrylic turntable that rotates the panel. My teammates built the electronics, which read humidity, wind speed, solar irradiance and dust.

6-DOF robotic arm (CAD)

Summer 2026
Team of 3 · UCLA MAE 94 · SolidWorks

A six-degree-of-freedom arm designed entirely in SolidWorks: 26 unique parts, 116 components, four subassemblies. I designed the base and lower-limb subassemblies, co-wrote the design concepts and specifications, and wrote the manufacturing and fabrication section of our report, including hole and shaft tolerances for 3D printing (±0.15 mm) versus CNC milling (±0.05 mm). It was a CAD-only course project.

Rover claw

Fall 2025
Team of 4 · UCLA engineering course · SolidWorks · 3D printing

The team built an autonomous mecanum-wheel rover with ultrasonic sensors. I modeled the claw: a servo turns a gear that converts its rotation into the straight-line motion that closes the claw. Getting the gear mesh clearance right took two printed iterations. A software issue kept the rover from completing its final run.
